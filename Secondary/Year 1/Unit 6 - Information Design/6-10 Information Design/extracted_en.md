Lesson introduction — Information Design

The websites and apps we use every day do not appear by accident — behind every design there are decisions to deliver the message correctly. Today we learn Information Design and how to organize and express information, how devices exchange it with us (UI) and how we feel it (UX).

Think of it this way:

Information Design is putting the right information in the right form — like a sign at a metro station that cannot be a long text, it must be a clear symbol everyone understands.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

Confusing UI with UX — the first is the interface you interact with, the second is the experience and feeling you get from using it.

Confusing Affordance with Signifier — the first is the possible actions on an object, the second is the cue that lets you know you can do the action.

Information Design

its three methods — book page 81

Information Design is the process of organizing and creatively expressing information according to its purpose, thereby ensuring that the intended message is correctly conveyed to the target audience.

There are basic methods used in Information Design: Abstraction, Visualization, and Structuring.

Abstraction

conveying the intended information simply from within a large amount of data

We summarize and extract only the important elements, and express them with symbols — among its most important symbols: the Pictogram, a pictorial symbol that conveys information without words used for signs at stations and airports, and the Icon, a representation of a program's content on a computer or smartphone through images or illustrations.

Visualization

visually representing information to make it more understandable

We convert information into pictures, drawings, and tables — like data tables and graphs.

Structuring

organizing information by relationships, connections, levels, stages, order, etc.

We arrange information into groups and levels — like hierarchical menus on web pages, and department store floor maps.

The User Interface

the medium between humans and devices — book page 81

A User Interface (UI) is a system for transferring information between humans and devices.

There are two main types: the Character User Interface (CUI) operated by entering commands via a keyboard, and the Graphical User Interface (GUI) that allows users to issue commands intuitively using icons and buttons.

Remember:

early computers used CUI with text, while current ones use GUI with icons and a mouse.

The User Experience

the experience and feelings — book page 81

The User Experience (UX) is the experience or emotional impact that users gain from interacting with a product or service.

The aim is not just ease of use, but also a sense of comfort — achieved by simplifying the interface, choosing colors with consideration for differences in perception, and adding helpful textual information.

Think of it this way:

UX is your whole 'feeling' about the product — happy? easy? fast? Even if you completed the task, the experience is what your brain records.

Affordance

the possible actions on an object — book page 81

Affordance is the actions or operations that can be performed on an object.

So the shape of an object hints at its possibilities: a raised button suggests it can be pressed, a handle suggests pulling or pushing, and a text field suggests typing into it.

Think of it this way:

The shape of a chair itself suggests you can sit on it — that is affordance. In design it is about things whose shape hints at their use without reading a manual.

The Signifier

the cues that prompt users to take action — book page 81

The Signifier is the cues that prompt users to take action.

If affordance is that a button can be pressed, then the signifier is what makes you notice it can be pressed — like a glowing arrow showing the scroll direction, a distinctive color, or text saying 'click here'.

The difference simply:

Affordance = the possible action (the button can be pressed). Signifier = the cue telling you to do it (the shape that lets you know).

### ✍️ Exercise 1: Information Design methods

Which is the most appropriate example of Abstraction as a method of Information Design?

A  a web page organized with menus for each hierarchy

B  pictograms were created for each classroom to guide people around the school

C  survey results were compiled into tables and graphs

✅ Answer: B

Pictograms are pictorial symbols that simply summarize information — that is Abstraction. The hierarchical page is Structuring, and tables are Visualization.

Which is the most appropriate example of Visualization?

A  text summarizing multiple matters in bullet points

B  a web page organized with menus for each hierarchy

C  survey results were compiled into tables and graphs

✅ Answer: C

Tables and graphs are examples of Visualization — visually representing information.

Which term fits: 'a method for extracting only the necessary elements from a large amount of information'?

A  Abstraction

B  Structuring

C  Visualization

✅ Answer: A — Abstraction

Abstraction conveys the intended information simply from within a large amount of data.
